Prices go up, salaries rise, shares fall. Percentage change is how we compare these moves fairly. The formula is short, but a few traps catch almost everyone at some point.

## The formula

**Percentage change = (new value − old value) ÷ old value × 100**

A **positive** answer is an increase, a **negative** answer is a decrease.

### Example: an increase

A monthly subscription goes from **$80 to $100**.

(100 − 80) ÷ 80 × 100 = 20 ÷ 80 × 100 = **25% increase**

### Example: a decrease

The same subscription drops from **$100 to $80**.

(80 − 100) ÷ 100 × 100 = −20 ÷ 100 × 100 = **−20%**, a 20% decrease

Notice that the difference is $20 both times, but the percentage isn't the same. That's the first trap.

## Mistake 1: using the wrong starting point

Always divide by the **original** value, the one you're changing *from*. That's why $80 → $100 is +25% but $100 → $80 is −20%.

## Mistake 2: thinking a rise and a fall cancel out

Suppose something worth **100** rises 10%, then falls 10%:

- After the rise: 100 × 1.10 = 110
- After the fall: 110 × 0.90 = **99**

You've lost 1%, because the 10% fall was taken from a bigger number. The effect grows with bigger moves: a 50% rise followed by a 50% fall leaves you at **75**, a quarter lower than where you started.

It works the same way for investments. **After a 25% fall, you need a 33.3% gain just to break even.**

## Mistake 3: mixing up percent and percentage points

If an interest rate goes from **4% to 5%**:

- It has risen by **1 percentage point** (5 − 4).
- It has risen by **25 percent** ((5 − 4) ÷ 4 × 100).

Both statements are true, but they mean very different things. News headlines often blur the two, so check which one is meant.

## Mistake 4: reversing a percentage the wrong way

A jacket costs **$60 after a 25% discount**. What was the original price?

It's tempting to add 25% back: $60 + $15 = $75. But that's wrong, because the 25% was taken off the *original* price, not $60.

You paid 75% of the original, so divide: **$60 ÷ 0.75 = $80**.

The same goes for tax: to remove 20% VAT from £120, divide by 1.20 to get £100, rather than subtracting 20% (which gives £96).

## Percentage change vs percentage difference

Use **percentage change** when one value clearly comes first (old vs new, before vs after).

Use **percentage difference** when you're comparing two values side by side, like two shops' prices. It divides by the average of the two:

**Percentage difference = |A − B| ÷ ((A + B) ÷ 2) × 100**

For 80 and 100: 20 ÷ 90 × 100 = **22.2%**, whichever way round you put them.

## Quick mental maths

- **10%** of anything: move the decimal point one place left. 10% of 250 is 25.
- **5%**: half of 10%. 5% of 250 is 12.5.
- **1%**: move the decimal point two places. 1% of 250 is 2.5.
- **25%**: divide by 4. **50%**: halve it.
- **Percentages are reversible:** 8% of 50 is the same as 50% of 8, which is 4.

## Worked example: a pay rise

Your salary goes from **$48,000 to $51,600**:

(51,600 − 48,000) ÷ 48,000 × 100 = 3,600 ÷ 48,000 × 100 = **7.5%**

If inflation that year was 3%, your pay rose by about 4.5% in real terms.

## Common questions

### How do I calculate a percentage change when the old value is zero?

You can't: dividing by zero has no answer. Going from 0 to 10 isn't a percentage increase; it's simply "up 10". In reports, people usually describe this as "new" or give the actual change instead of a percentage.

### How do I calculate percentage change with negative numbers?

Divide by the size of the old value, ignoring its sign. A loss that shrinks from −50 to −25 is an improvement of (−25 − (−50)) ÷ 50 × 100 = **+50%**. Dividing by −50 would wrongly show it as a decrease.

### How do I find the new value after a percentage change?

Multiply the old value by 1 plus the change as a decimal. A 15% increase on 200 is 200 × 1.15 = 230. A 15% decrease is 200 × 0.85 = 170. For several changes in a row, multiply by each factor in turn.

## Key takeaways

- Divide by the original value: (new − old) ÷ old × 100.
- A rise and a fall of the same percentage don't cancel out.
- Percentage points and percent are different things.
- To undo a percentage, divide; don't add it back.
