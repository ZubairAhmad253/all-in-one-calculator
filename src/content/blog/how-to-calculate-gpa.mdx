Your GPA (grade point average) sums up your academic performance in one number. It matters for scholarships, graduate school and some job applications, so it's worth knowing exactly how it works, and how much you can still change it.

## Step 1: turn letter grades into grade points

Most US colleges use a **4.0 scale**:

| Letter | Points |
| --- | --- |
| A | 4.0 |
| A− | 3.7 |
| B+ | 3.3 |
| B | 3.0 |
| B− | 2.7 |
| C+ | 2.3 |
| C | 2.0 |
| C− | 1.7 |
| D+ | 1.3 |
| D | 1.0 |
| D− | 0.7 |
| F | 0.0 |

Some schools give an **A+ 4.3**, while most cap it at 4.0. Check your school's policy.

## Step 2: multiply by credit hours

Courses with more credit hours count more. Multiply each course's grade points by its credits to get its **quality points**.

## Step 3: divide by total credits

**GPA = total quality points ÷ total credits**

### Worked example

| Course | Grade | Points | Credits | Quality points |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Biology | A | 4.0 | 3 | 12.0 |
| Calculus | B+ | 3.3 | 4 | 13.2 |
| History | B | 3.0 | 3 | 9.0 |
| Art | C | 2.0 | 2 | 4.0 |
| **Total** | | | **12** | **38.2** |

38.2 ÷ 12 = **3.18 GPA**

Notice how the 4-credit calculus course has the biggest influence. A B+ there moves your GPA more than an A in a 1-credit class would.

## Cumulative GPA

Your **cumulative GPA** covers every term so far. To update it, don't just average the term GPAs; weight them by credits:

**Cumulative GPA = (old GPA × old credits + new quality points) ÷ (old credits + new credits)**

If you have a **3.0 GPA over 60 credits** and earn a **4.0 in 15 new credits**:

(3.0 × 60 + 4.0 × 15) ÷ 75 = (180 + 60) ÷ 75 = **3.20**

## How much can you raise your GPA?

The more credits you've already completed, the harder your GPA is to move. The same excellent term has a very different effect depending on how far along you are:

| Starting point | After a 4.0 term of 15 credits |
| --- | --- |
| 3.0 over 15 credits | 3.50 |
| 3.0 over 60 credits | 3.20 |
| 3.0 over 90 credits | 3.14 |

**The early years matter most.** If you're a first-year student, a strong semester can change your GPA dramatically. Later on, progress is slower, so set realistic goals.

## Realistic ways to raise your GPA

- **Focus on high-credit courses:** they carry the most weight.
- **Check your school's retake policy:** some replace a low grade when you retake the course.
- **Use pass/fail wisely** for courses outside your major, if your school allows it and it won't hurt your plans.
- **Talk to your professors early,** not after the final.
- **Know what you need:** work out the score you need on each final and plan your revision around it.
- **Balance your schedule:** avoid stacking several of your hardest courses in the same term.

## Weighted vs unweighted GPA

High schools often use a **weighted GPA**, adding extra points for honors, AP or IB classes (so an A might be worth 5.0). Colleges may recalculate your GPA on an unweighted 4.0 scale, so it's worth knowing both.

## Common questions

### Do pass/fail courses count towards my GPA?

Usually not. A "pass" earns credits but no grade points, so it doesn't change your GPA. A "fail" may or may not count, depending on your school, so check your student handbook.

### What happens to my GPA if I retake a course?

It depends on your school's policy. Some replace the old grade with the new one; others average the two or count both. Grade replacement can raise your GPA a lot, especially if the original grade was an F in a high-credit course.

### What is a good GPA for graduate school?

Many graduate programmes look for at least a 3.0, and competitive programmes often expect 3.5 or higher. Your GPA in your major and in your last two years often matters more than your overall number, so a strong upward trend helps.

## Key takeaways

- GPA = quality points ÷ credits, where quality points = grade points × credits.
- Higher-credit courses move your GPA the most.
- Cumulative GPA is credit-weighted, and it gets harder to move over time.
