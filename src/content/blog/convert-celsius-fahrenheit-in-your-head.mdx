Travelling, following a recipe from another country, or checking a child's temperature: at some point you'll need to switch between Celsius and Fahrenheit. Here's the exact method and a shortcut you can do in your head.

## The exact formulas

**Celsius to Fahrenheit:** °F = °C × 9/5 + 32

**Fahrenheit to Celsius:** °C = (°F − 32) × 5/9

9/5 is 1.8, so you can also write the first as °F = °C × 1.8 + 32.

### Examples

- **25 °C:** 25 × 1.8 + 32 = 45 + 32 = **77 °F**
- **100 °F:** (100 − 32) × 5/9 = 68 × 5/9 ≈ **37.8 °C**

## The mental shortcut

For everyday temperatures, use this:

**Celsius to Fahrenheit: double it and add 30.**
**Fahrenheit to Celsius: subtract 30 and halve it.**

How close is it?

| Celsius | Quick estimate | Exact |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 0 °C | 30 °F | 32 °F |
| 10 °C | 50 °F | 50 °F |
| 15 °C | 60 °F | 59 °F |
| 20 °C | 70 °F | 68 °F |
| 25 °C | 80 °F | 77 °F |
| 30 °C | 90 °F | 86 °F |
| 35 °C | 100 °F | 95 °F |

Between about 0 and 25 °C, the shortcut is within about 3 degrees, which is plenty for deciding whether to take a jacket. It drifts further off at high and low temperatures, so **don't use it for cooking or medical readings**.

## Memorable reference points

Learning a few anchors makes temperatures feel familiar:

- **−40:** the one temperature that's the same in both scales.
- **0 °C = 32 °F:** water freezes.
- **10 °C = 50 °F:** a cool day.
- **20 °C = 68 °F:** comfortable room temperature.
- **30 °C = 86 °F:** a hot summer day.
- **37 °C = 98.6 °F:** average body temperature.
- **100 °C = 212 °F:** water boils at sea level.

A handy rhyme for the warm end: **"30 is hot, 20 is nice, 10 is cold, 0 is ice."**

## Body temperature

- **Normal:** around 36.1–37.2 °C (97–99 °F).
- **Fever:** 38 °C (100.4 °F) or higher is generally considered a fever.

For health readings, always use the exact conversion or a thermometer set to your preferred scale.

## Oven temperatures

Recipes round oven temperatures to convenient numbers, so conversions in cookbooks aren't exact:

| Celsius | Recipe °F | Gas mark |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 150 °C | 300 °F | 2 |
| 180 °C | 350 °F | 4 |
| 200 °C | 400 °F | 6 |
| 220 °C | 425 °F | 7 |

For a **fan (convection) oven**, lower the temperature by about 20 °C (25 °F).

## What about Kelvin?

Scientists use **Kelvin**, which starts at absolute zero, the coldest possible temperature. To convert, add 273.15 to the Celsius figure: 20 °C = 293.15 K. Kelvin has no degree sign and is never negative.

## Why two scales?

Celsius sets 0 and 100 at the freezing and boiling points of water, so it fits neatly with the metric system and is used almost everywhere. Fahrenheit, used mainly in the United States, has smaller degrees: a 1 °C change is a 1.8 °F change, which some people like for describing the weather.

## Common questions

### Why does the shortcut get less accurate at extreme temperatures?

The exact conversion multiplies by 1.8, while the shortcut doubles. The difference is small at 10 or 20 degrees but grows as the number gets bigger. At 40 °C the shortcut says 110 °F, while the true value is 104 °F.

### Is there a more accurate quick method?

Yes: double the Celsius figure, **subtract 10%** of that, then add 32. For 30 °C: 60 − 6 + 32 = **86 °F**, which is exact. Going the other way, subtract 32, halve it, then add about 10% of that result: 86 °F gives 54 ÷ 2 = 27, plus 2.7 = **29.7 °C**, very close to the true 30 °C.

### Which countries use Fahrenheit?

The United States, along with a few smaller territories and countries, uses Fahrenheit for everyday temperatures. Nearly everywhere else uses Celsius, and scientists everywhere use Celsius or Kelvin.

## Key takeaways

- Exact: °F = °C × 1.8 + 32, and °C = (°F − 32) ÷ 1.8.
- Quick: double and add 30 (or subtract 30 and halve).
- Use exact conversions for cooking, science and health.
