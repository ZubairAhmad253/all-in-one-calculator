You have money to invest in mutual funds. Should you put it all in at once (a **lump sum**) or spread it out month by month (a **SIP**, or systematic investment plan)? The honest answer is: it depends on whether you have the money now, and how you feel about market ups and downs.

## The two approaches

- **Lump sum:** invest the whole amount on day one.
- **SIP:** invest a fixed amount every month, usually by automatic debit.

Most people who invest through a SIP do so because they're **investing from their monthly income**, not because they're holding back a lump sum. That's the most important point in this whole debate.

## A worked comparison

Suppose you invest **₹6,00,000 in total over 10 years** and the fund returns a steady **12% a year**.

| | Lump sum | SIP |
| --- | --- | --- |
| How you invest | ₹6,00,000 on day one | ₹5,000 a month for 10 years |
| Total invested | ₹6,00,000 | ₹6,00,000 |
| Value after 10 years | ₹18,63,509 | ₹11,61,695 |

The lump sum ends up much higher. That's not because the SIP is worse; it's because **the lump-sum money is invested for longer**. Every rupee goes in on day one, while the SIP's last instalment is invested for only a month.

So if you already have the money and markets rise steadily, investing it all at once usually comes out ahead.

## But markets don't rise steadily

Real markets go up and down, and that's where a SIP helps. When you invest a fixed amount every month, you **automatically buy more units when prices are low and fewer when they are high**. This is called **rupee cost averaging**.

Here's a simple example. You invest **₹1,000 a month** for five months while the fund's price moves around:

| Month | Unit price | Units bought |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | ₹100 | 10.0 |
| 2 | ₹80 | 12.5 |
| 3 | ₹50 | 20.0 |
| 4 | ₹80 | 12.5 |
| 5 | ₹100 | 10.0 |
| **Total** | | **65.0 units** |

You invested ₹5,000 and bought 65 units, an **average cost of ₹76.92 per unit**, even though the average price over those months was ₹82. When the price is back at ₹100, your units are worth **₹6,500**: a 30% gain although the price ended exactly where it started.

The fall in month 3 hurt a lump-sum investor, but it helped the SIP investor, who bought twice as many units that month.

## Which is better for you?

**A SIP suits you if:**

- You're investing from your **monthly salary**.
- You'd be **nervous investing a large amount** just before a market fall.
- You want a **habit** that runs on autopilot.

**A lump sum suits you if:**

- You already have the money, for example a bonus or inheritance.
- You have a **long time horizon** (7–10 years or more).
- You can **stay invested calmly** if the value drops 20–30% soon after.

**A middle path:** if you have a lump sum but worry about timing, spread it over 6–12 months with a **systematic transfer plan (STP)** from a liquid or debt fund into an equity fund.

## Supercharge a SIP with a step-up

As your income grows, raise your SIP. Increasing ₹5,000 a month by **10% every year** for the same 10 years means investing ₹9,56,245 in total, and at 12% it could grow to about **₹16,87,163**. Many platforms let you set the step-up automatically.

## Common questions

### Can I stop or pause a SIP?

Yes. Most SIPs can be paused or stopped at any time without a penalty, although some funds charge an exit load if you withdraw within a short period (often one year). Pausing during a market fall is usually a mistake, though: those are the months when your fixed amount buys the most units.

### Which date of the month is best for a SIP?

Over a long period, the date makes very little difference. Pick a day shortly after your salary arrives so the money is always there and you never miss an instalment.

### Is a SIP the same as a recurring deposit?

No. A recurring deposit (RD) pays a fixed, guaranteed interest rate. A SIP invests in a mutual fund, so its value rises and falls with the market. SIPs in equity funds have the potential for higher long-term returns, but with more risk.

## What matters more than the method

- **Starting early:** time in the market beats timing the market.
- **Staying invested** through the dips.
- **Choosing suitable, low-cost funds** for your goal and risk level.
- **Reviewing once a year,** not every day.

Remember that returns aren't guaranteed. These examples assume a steady 12%, but real returns vary from year to year.
