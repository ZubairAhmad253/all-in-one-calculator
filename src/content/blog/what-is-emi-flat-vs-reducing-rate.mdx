If you've taken a home, car or personal loan in India, Pakistan or much of Asia, you'll have heard the word **EMI**. It's the single number most people look at when choosing a loan, but on its own it can hide how much the loan really costs.

## What EMI means

EMI stands for **Equated Monthly Instalment**: the fixed amount you pay your lender every month until the loan is repaid. Each EMI does two jobs:

1. It pays that month's **interest**.
2. The rest reduces the **principal** you owe.

## How EMI is calculated

Banks use the reducing-balance formula:

**EMI = P × r × (1 + r)ⁿ ÷ ((1 + r)ⁿ − 1)**

- **P** is the loan amount
- **r** is the monthly interest rate (annual rate ÷ 12 ÷ 100)
- **n** is the number of monthly instalments

For a **₹10,00,000 loan at 10% for 20 years**, r = 10 ÷ 12 ÷ 100 ≈ 0.00833 and n = 240, which gives an EMI of **₹9,650**. Over 20 years you'd pay **₹13,16,052 in interest**, more than the loan itself.

You don't need to do this by hand: the [EMI calculator](/emi-calculator) works it out instantly and shows the full repayment schedule.

## Flat rate vs reducing balance

Some lenders, especially for car and personal loans, quote a **flat rate**. It sounds cheaper than it is.

- **Reducing balance:** interest is charged only on what you still owe. As you repay, the interest goes down.
- **Flat rate:** interest is charged on the **original** loan amount for the whole tenure, even though you're paying it back every month.

### The same "10%", two very different costs

Take a **₹10,00,000 loan for 5 years at 10%**:

| | Flat rate 10% | Reducing balance 10% |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Total interest | ₹5,00,000 | ₹2,74,823 |
| Monthly EMI | ₹25,000 | ₹21,247 |

With the flat rate, you pay **₹2,25,177 more** for the same loan. In fact, a 10% flat rate over 5 years costs the same as about **17.3% on a reducing balance**.

**Always ask for the reducing-balance rate (or the APR)** when comparing loans. If a lender only gives a flat rate, convert it before deciding.

## How tenure changes your EMI

A longer tenure lowers the EMI, but you pay interest for longer. For ₹10,00,000 at 10%:

| Tenure | EMI | Total interest |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 5 years | ₹21,247 | ₹2,74,823 |
| 10 years | ₹13,215 | ₹5,85,809 |
| 15 years | ₹10,746 | ₹9,34,289 |
| 20 years | ₹9,650 | ₹13,16,052 |

Going from 10 to 20 years lowers the EMI by about ₹3,565, but more than **doubles the interest**. Choose the shortest tenure whose EMI you can comfortably afford every month.

## Why early EMIs are mostly interest

Because interest is charged on the outstanding balance, the early EMIs are mostly interest and the later ones mostly principal. In the 20-year example, the first EMI of ₹9,650 includes about ₹8,333 of interest, so only around ₹1,317 reduces the loan.

This is also why **part-prepayments early in the loan save the most**: every rupee you prepay stops attracting interest for the rest of the tenure.

## Prepayment: reduce EMI or reduce tenure?

When you make a part-prepayment, most banks let you choose:

- **Reduce the tenure** (same EMI, loan ends sooner). This saves the most interest.
- **Reduce the EMI** (same end date, smaller instalments). This gives you more breathing room each month.

If your budget is comfortable, reducing the tenure is usually the better deal.

## How much EMI can you afford?

A common guideline is to keep **all your EMIs together under 40–50% of your take-home pay**, and lower if your income is irregular. Leave room for savings and emergencies too.

## Common questions

### Does a lower EMI always mean a cheaper loan?

No. A lower EMI usually comes from a longer tenure, which means paying interest for more years. The 20-year loan in our example has the lowest EMI but more than twice the total interest of the 10-year loan. Always compare the **total amount payable**, not just the monthly figure.

### What happens to my EMI if interest rates change?

On a **floating-rate** loan, your bank may change the rate when its benchmark changes. Many banks then keep your EMI the same and extend the tenure, so a rate rise can quietly add years to your loan. Ask your bank which option it uses, and consider asking for a higher EMI instead to keep the original end date.

### Is it worth prepaying a loan in its last few years?

Usually less so. By then most of each EMI is principal, so there's little interest left to save. Prepaying early in the loan gives the biggest benefit; late in the loan, that money may do more good elsewhere, such as in an emergency fund or an investment.

## Checklist before you sign

- Compare the **reducing-balance rate or APR**, not the flat rate.
- Add **processing fees and insurance** to the total cost.
- Check **prepayment and foreclosure charges**.
- For floating-rate loans, test your EMI at a rate **1–2% higher** to be safe.
