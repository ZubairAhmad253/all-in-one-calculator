Compound interest is often called the most powerful force in personal finance. The idea is simple: **you earn interest on your interest**. Given enough time, that small twist makes an enormous difference.

## Simple vs compound interest

With **simple interest**, you only ever earn interest on the money you put in. With **compound interest**, each period's interest is added to your balance, so the next period's interest is calculated on a bigger number.

Here's **$10,000 at 7% a year for 30 years**:

| Type | Balance after 30 years |
| --- | --- |
| Simple interest | $31,000 |
| Compound interest (yearly) | $76,123 |

Same money, same rate, same time. Compounding more than doubles the result, because in the later years you're earning interest on tens of thousands of dollars of past interest.

## The compound interest formula

**A = P × (1 + r ÷ n)^(n × t)**

- **A**: the final amount
- **P**: the starting amount
- **r**: the annual interest rate as a decimal (7% = 0.07)
- **n**: how many times a year interest is compounded
- **t**: the number of years

## The Rule of 72

Want a quick answer without a calculator? **Divide 72 by the interest rate** to estimate how many years it takes for money to double.

| Annual rate | Rule of 72 | Exact answer |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 4% | 18 years | 17.7 years |
| 6% | 12 years | 11.9 years |
| 8% | 9 years | 9.0 years |
| 10% | 7.2 years | 7.3 years |
| 12% | 6 years | 6.1 years |

It's remarkably accurate for everyday rates. It works in reverse too: to double your money in 10 years, you need about 72 ÷ 10 = 7.2% a year.

The Rule of 72 also warns you about debt. A credit card at 24% doubles what you owe in about **three years** if you don't pay it down.

## Why starting early beats saving more

Time is the biggest ingredient in compounding. Compare two savers who each earn 7% a year, compounded monthly:

- **Early Emma** saves **$200 a month for 40 years** (from 25 to 65). She puts in $96,000.
- **Late Liam** saves **$200 a month for 30 years** (from 35 to 65). He puts in $72,000.

| | Paid in | Balance at 65 |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Emma (40 years) | $96,000 | $524,963 |
| Liam (30 years) | $72,000 | $243,994 |

Emma paid in only $24,000 more, but ends up with **over twice as much**. Even if Liam saves **$300 a month** for his 30 years (paying in $108,000, more than Emma), he reaches only about **$365,991**.

Those extra ten years at the start are worth more than a bigger monthly amount later.

## How often interest compounds

Interest can be added yearly, quarterly, monthly or daily. More frequent compounding earns a little more, which is why banks advertise the **APY** (annual percentage yield), the real yearly return once compounding is included. A 5% rate compounded monthly gives an APY of 5.116%.

In practice, the difference between monthly and daily compounding is small. **The rate and the time invested matter far more** than the compounding frequency.

## Making compounding work for you

- **Start now,** even with a small amount.
- **Automate a monthly deposit** so you never skip it.
- **Reinvest your returns** instead of spending them.
- **Keep costs low:** a 1% yearly fee compounds against you just as returns compound for you.
- **Pay off high-interest debt first:** it compounds too, in the wrong direction.

## Common questions

### Does compound interest work on debt too?

Yes, and that's why credit card debt grows so quickly. Unpaid interest is added to the balance, and next month's interest is charged on the larger amount. Paying only the minimum can keep you in debt for many years. Clearing high-interest debt is usually the best "investment" you can make.

### What's a realistic rate of return to use?

Savings accounts pay whatever your bank offers, often a few percent. For long-term stock market investing, many people plan with 5–7% a year after inflation, but returns vary widely from year to year and aren't guaranteed. When in doubt, test a lower rate too, so your plan still works if returns disappoint.

### How does inflation affect compound interest?

Inflation reduces what your money can buy. If your savings earn 5% and prices rise 3%, your real growth is only about 2% a year. To see roughly what your future balance is worth in today's money, subtract the expected inflation rate from your interest rate before you calculate.

## Key takeaways

- Compound interest earns interest on your interest; simple interest doesn't.
- The Rule of 72 tells you roughly how long it takes to double your money: 72 ÷ rate.
- Starting ten years earlier can beat saving 50% more per month.
